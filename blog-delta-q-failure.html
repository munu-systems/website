ΔQ, modal mu-calculus, and the return to determinism

25 MAY 2026 · STEWART MACKENZIE

The previous two posts established the mu/nu duality and the case for modal mu-calculus as the language for behaviour clauses. This post ties them together with a third piece: ΔQ, the quality-centric systems paradigm developed by Neil Davies and Peter Thompson at Predictable Network Solutions. The combination of ΔQ and modal mu-calculus in Munu's do and must clauses is not an accident — it's the theoretically correct way to build distributed fault-tolerant systems that maintain deterministic execution.

The mass metaphor

ΔQ models every computation as mass that must be delivered. When you spawn a process to compute a result, that result has mass — it's something the system has committed to producing. The mass can be in one of three states:

Tangible — the computation is in flight or has been delivered. This is the happy path. The result either hasn't arrived yet (the variable is unbound) or it has arrived (the variable is bound). Mass is on its way to where it needs to be.

Attenuated — the result was delivered, but at degraded quality. The computation took longer than expected, or the supervisor had to absorb a crash and return a diagnostic instead of the real result. The mass arrived, but it's not what was hoped for. In ΔQ's terms, the quality was attenuated at a mitigation point.

Intangible — the mass is permanently lost. The computation will never produce a result. The variable will never be bound. This is the state from which there is no recovery — it can only cascade upward through the supervision tree.

The key insight of ΔQ is that these transitions are monotonic. Mass moves toward delivery or loss. You can attenuate quality — deliver a degraded result — but you cannot un-deliver or un-lose. This monotonicity is what makes the model compositional: the ΔQ of a composite system can be computed from the ΔQs of its parts.

Where Collet meets ΔQ

Raphaël Collet's 2007 thesis, The Limits of Network Transparency in a Distributed Programming Language, introduced asynchronous failure handling for the Oz/Mozart distributed programming system. Joe Armstrong — the creator of Erlang — sat on the thesis jury, which tells you something about the calibre of the work and the directness of the lineage between Erlang's fault tolerance philosophy and Collet's formalisation of it. His key contribution was the fault stream — an asynchronous, separate channel through which failures are observed, decoupled from the data variables they affect.

Collet's model defines three failure detectors — tempFail, permFail, and localFail — each with distinct properties of completeness, accuracy, and monotonicity. The tempFail detector is non-monotonic: it can suspect a process and then change its mind. The permFail detector is monotonic and accurate: once it declares failure, the declaration is final and never wrong. This maps directly to the ΔQ mass states: tempFail is mass that might still arrive (tangible but delayed), while permFail is mass that's gone (intangible).

In Munu, these two frameworks are not two subsystems bolted together. The supervisor is both the ΔQ mitigation point and the Collet failure authority. When a supervisor absorbs a child crash — binding the result variable to a diagnostic string — it is simultaneously performing Collet's asynchronous failure handling and ΔQ's quality attenuation at the mitigation point. One mechanism, one entity, two theoretical frameworks unified.

The two-level quality authority

This is where the do and must clauses map onto the failure model with precision that goes beyond what either Collet or ΔQ alone can express.

The do clause: self-measurement

The do clause is the process's own observation of its behaviour. When a process violates its do formula — it crashes, times out, produces an unexpected event — it's performing self-measurement of quality. In ΔQ terms, the process is saying "I know my output is wrong." In Collet's terms, the process's own coalgebra fires first.

A do violation triggers TempFail. The mass stays tangible — the supervisor can restart the process, and the result variable remains unbound, waiting for a new attempt. The system has detected non-determinism and is taking the fast path back to a known state: kill the process, restart it, try again. This is Erlang's "let it crash" philosophy expressed as a temporal property.

The must clause: authority measurement

The must clause is the supervisor's obligation on the supervised process. It specifies what the process must do — not what happens when it fails, but what properties its execution must satisfy. When a process violates its must formula, the supervisor declares PermFail. The mass becomes intangible.

This distinction matters enormously. A do violation is the process admitting it entered a non-deterministic state — and the response is to try to recover determinism through restart. A must violation is the authority declaring that the process has breached its contract — and the response is permanent failure, because the process has demonstrated that it cannot be trusted to return to determinism.

TempFail means the system can try again. PermFail means it must not.

CDF breaching as the correct failure signal

This is where ΔQ and modal mu-calculus fuse in a way that neither framework achieves alone.

ΔQ models quality attenuation as an Improper Random Variable (IRV) — a cumulative distribution function (CDF) whose total probability is less than 1. The gap between the CDF's limit and 1.0 represents the probability of failure — mass that will never arrive. This is not a timeout. It's a probabilistic envelope: "95% of the time, this operation completes within 100ms; 99% of the time, within 500ms."

In Munu, these envelopes are attached to behaviour formulas using the ~ operator:

nu Y. [fail(self, _)]~fast_response <restart(self)> Y

The ~fast_response is a CDF declaration: cdf fast_response = [(100ms, 0.95), (500ms, 0.99)]. It says: if a fail event occurs, it must occur within this timing envelope. Not "within 500ms" — within a distribution of times, because real systems don't have hard deadlines, they have probabilistic ones.

Breaching a CDF is the correct signal for TempFail. Not a timeout — a statistical violation. The process is still running, but it's outside the envelope of expected behaviour. It has entered a non-deterministic regime where its timing is no longer predictable. The supervisor puts it into TempFail and restarts: mass stays tangible, but the process that's producing it gets a fresh start.

Breaching the must formula is the correct signal for PermFail. The process didn't just run slowly — it did something it was contractually forbidden to do. The authority has spoken: this mass is intangible. Cascade the failure upward.

Non-determinism as the unifying enemy

Every concept in this model is a weapon against non-determinism:

Source of non-determinismHow Munu constrains it
Message orderingSingle-assignment dataflow variables. Reads block until bound. No mailbox, no ordering ambiguity.
Timing uncertaintyΔQ CDFs on behaviour modalities. The acceptable timing distribution is part of the contract.
Process failureCollet-style fault streams, observed by the supervisor's coalgebra. Failure is asynchronous and separate from data.
Recovery uncertaintyModal mu-calculus do formulas. The recovery protocol is a temporal property, not ad-hoc code.
Contract violationModal mu-calculus must formulas. Breach → PermFail. The process is removed from the system.
Cascading failureΔQ mass monotonicity. Intangible mass propagates upward through the supervision tree until an ancestor absorbs or the root accepts.

The modal mu-calculus is what ties all of this together. It's the language that lets you draw the line between deterministic and non-deterministic execution. Everything the formula accepts is within the deterministic envelope — the system is behaving as specified. Everything the formula rejects is non-deterministic — the system has left the space of acceptable behaviour, and the response is to return to determinism as fast as possible: restart (TempFail) if recovery is possible, escalate (PermFail) if it isn't.

This is why modal mu-calculus is the correct choice, not just a convenient one. It is the most expressive temporal logic in the branching-time hierarchy. It can express any property that CTL*, LTL, or Hennessy-Milner logic can express — and properties that none of them can. When you need to specify the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable behaviour in a distributed system, you need a logic that can express every temporal property you might care about. Modal mu-calculus is that logic.

The absorb operation

There is one more piece that makes the unification concrete: the absorb operation.

When a child process crashes and the supervisor decides to absorb the failure, the kernel does something remarkable: it binds the child's result variable to a crash diagnostic. Not an error code. Not an exception. A bound variable — the same bind() operation that a successful computation uses.

The parent thread, suspended on that variable, wakes up and gets a value. It does not know whether the value came from successful completion or from a formatted crash diagnostic. The variable carries no "this was an error" metadata. It is just a bound string.

This is ΔQ's quality attenuation made concrete. The mass was heading toward intangible — the child crashed, the result was about to be lost. The supervisor, acting as both the Collet failure authority and the ΔQ mitigation point, intervened. It converted intangible mass back to tangible mass at degraded quality. The result arrived, but it's a crash diagnostic instead of the computed value. Attenuated, but delivered.

The parent can now make its own decision about what to do with the attenuated result — retry with different parameters, fall back to a cached value, propagate the diagnostic upstream. The non-determinism has been contained: the system has returned to a deterministic state where every variable is either bound or permanently failed, and every process knows which.

Determinism is not the absence of failure. It's the property that every failure has a specified, verifiable response.
